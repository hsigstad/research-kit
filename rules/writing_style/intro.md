## Choosing the research question

Before writing the intro, check the question itself. Shapiro's three
criteria for a good applied-econ research question:

- **Has an answer.** We may not know what it is, but we can agree that
  an answer exists. *"What is the effect on quantity sold of imposing a
  price ceiling on ketchup?"* has an answer. *"What is the nature of
  firm and consumer behavior in the ketchup industry?"* does not.
- **Answer is not obvious.** We cannot determine it from logic, common
  sense, or existing evidence. *"Would making ketchup available only on
  Tuesdays improve consumer welfare?"* has an answer, but we can
  probably guess what it is.
- **Answer is actionable.** If we knew the answer, someone (a
  policymaker, firm, NGO) might do things differently.

If the question fails any of these, restate it before writing the intro.
A good intro cannot rescue a question that has no answer, an obvious
one, or a non-actionable one.

## The recipe

The introduction follows a known recipe. Keith Head's "Introduction
Formula" is the canonical econ reference; Jesse Shapiro's "How to Present
an Applied Micro Paper" is the related slide deck. A typical applied-paper
intro has roughly these elements, in order:

1. **Hook (¶1).** Open with concrete material — a specific case, a vivid
   empirical fact, a sharp tension, or a principle stated with specific
   stakes the reader can picture. Avoid abstract framings ("understanding
   X is essential for Y"). The hook can pair a body-level principle with
   a footnote-anchored case (the pattern in *Lambais & Sigstad 2023* and
   *Chin, Lambais & Sigstad*: principle in the body, real case in a
   footnote).
2. **Research question (¶1 or ¶2).** What does this paper ask? State as
   a question or "we study X". Motivated by the hook.
3. **Why this is hard / antecedents (¶2 or ¶3).** What's been done? Why
   doesn't it fully answer the question? Frame as "what we don't yet
   know", not as a passive lit review.
4. **Setting and data (¶3).** What gives a sharp view of the question?
   Why this setting?
5. **Approach (¶4–5).** Method, model, identification.
6. **Results (¶5–7).** Main findings in 2–4 paragraphs. Lead with the
   headline.
7. **Mechanism / interpretation (¶8).** Why do the results say what we
   claim? What rules out alternatives?
8. **Welfare / policy implications (¶9, optional).**
9. **Literature contributions.** Frame as "this paper contributes to X
   by …" for three or four threads. Each thread sets the paper off
   against the 2–3 closest current papers — not a JEL-style review of
   the whole literature (Cochrane). Place **near the end** of the
   intro, not at the start.
10. **Roadmap (last paragraph).** "Section 2 describes … Section 3
    presents …"

## Shapiro's 15-paragraph template (concrete)

Jesse Shapiro's "Four Steps to an Applied Micro Paper" gives a more
granular paragraph-by-paragraph template that maps to the elements
above:

- **¶1–2: Motivation.** A reader in any field of econ should believe
  that answering the research question would be an important
  contribution.
- **¶3–4: Challenges.** Why has the question not already been answered?
  What are the central difficulties a researcher must tackle?
- **¶5: "This paper".** What the paper accomplishes and how, in a
  nutshell.
- **¶6–7: Model.** Key formal assumptions you maintain.
- **¶8–9: Data.** Where the data come from; how key concepts are
  measured.
- **¶10–11: Methods.** How you take the model to the data; how you
  overcome the challenges from ¶3–4.
- **¶12–13: Findings.** Key findings; connect them clearly back to the
  motivation in ¶1–2.
- **¶14–15: Literature.** Two contributions, one paragraph each, each
  explaining how the paper differs from the closest recent work.

Use the template as a guide, not a rigid mold — paragraphs may compress
or expand by section. The order matters more than the exact count.

## Hard rules

- **Don't open with the literature gap.** "A large literature on X
  documents Y; we extend it by Z" puts the lit review before the
  question. The lit gap comes after the question, framed as what we
  don't yet know.
- **Don't open with the contribution.** "We construct …" / "We develop
  …" / "We propose …" comes after the question is established.
- **The hook must be concrete.** A reader who has never seen this paper
  should know in the first 30 seconds what it is about. Generic abstract
  framing fails this test.
- **Place the literature-contributions paragraph near the end, not at
  the start.** The intro is read top-to-bottom; readers care about your
  question and findings before they care which threads you contribute
  to.
- **Question-first applies to the intro too.** The opening sentence is
  the question or its concrete motivation, not the contribution and not
  the lit gap. Same rule as `abstract.md`.
- **Don't open with a cute quotation.** No epigraphs from literature,
  song lyrics, or famous figures.
- **Submission discipline: contractual, not aspirational.** Shapiro's
  step 1 (aspirational intro — write the intro for the paper you wish
  you had) is a thinking tool for early-stage project formulation. By
  the time the paper is shared externally — a conference, working
  paper, journal submission, or coauthors not involved in formulation —
  the intro must be contractual: every claim backed by what the paper
  actually delivers. An intro that promises more than the body delivers
  signals that the writing process is incomplete (general baseline §14).
  When validating a near-submission draft, flag aspirational claims as
  HIGH severity — they are commitments the paper hasn't earned.

## Henrik's preferred opener pattern

Lead with a concrete principle that names the stakes specifically
(avoiding abstract framings), and anchor a real case in the first
footnote of ¶1 if the case illustrates the principle. This is the
pattern in his published applied work.
